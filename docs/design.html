What measurement changed

correction

The original design here argued for a full inference engine built on co-activation-aware disk layout, projecting 8–12 tok/s for a 30B-class MoE. Three findings, in order, cut that down:

  • Layout is the secondary lever, not the primary one. Once you issue a layer's top-k reads concurrently — which any competent engine does — the measured gain from reordering experts is modest. Queue depth is worth 6–8× on the same device and no file layout can influence it. The big lever belongs to a runtime.
  • Fixed 2 MiB groups you always read whole are a net loss. The over-read costs more than the coalescing saves at any expert size above ~256 KiB. What actually pays is coalescing only the slices you need into contiguous runs.
  • The runtime side is already occupied. Colibri (25.7k stars, pure C, Apache-2.0) ships expert streaming, router-lookahead prefetch, learned hot-expert pinning and speculative decoding. Building a fourth engine would have duplicated mature work.

So the project became a layout compiler instead: it rewrites the byte layout of a GGUF checkpoint, provably without touching a weight, and composes with every existing runtime rather than competing. Crucially it measures the gain for a given model, trace and device rather than asserting one — and reports honestly when the answer is "leave it alone". Sections 04–09 below are superseded on the engine question; sections 01–03 stand.

01

The quantity that actually binds

Every "run big models locally" project starts from the wrong quantity. Parameter count is not what your laptop runs out of. What binds is bytes of weight touched per generated token — and those two numbers are only equal for a dense model held entirely in RAM, which is the one configuration we have no intention of using.

Once you separate them, the problem factors cleanly. Bytes per token is a product of four independent terms, and each has its own literature:

bytes/token = (params) × (bits per param / 8) × (fraction touched per token) ÷ (tokens accepted per forward pass)

A 30B model at 4 bits that touches 10% of its weights per token and accepts 6 tokens per pass moves 250 MB per token, not 60 GB. That is a number an SSD can serve. The engineering question is whether you can actually collect those scattered 250 MB at the disk's rated speed — and that turns out to be where every existing tool falls apart.

02

Three measurements that reframe the problem

The received wisdom is that disk-backed inference is hopeless. That wisdom comes from GPU-land, where you compare NVMe against HBM. On a RAM-poor CPU laptop you are comparing NVMe against DDR, and the ratio is completely different. I measured it directly.

Two hard constraints fall straight out of that table, and they are the reason this design looks the way it does.

  • Queue depth is worth 6–8×. At 256 KiB, one outstanding read gives 0.35 GB/s and eight give 1.82. Nothing about the kernel changed — only how many reads were in flight. This is the dominant lever, and it is a property of the runtime: a file cannot make its reader queue more deeply.
  • Request size is worth 7×, but only below ~256 KiB. At QD8, 16 KiB gives 0.25 GB/s and 256 KiB gives 1.82. Above 256 KiB the surface plateaus, so for coarse MoE checkpoints layout barely matters. This is the lever a file can pull, and it is why potatomaxx targets coalescing rather than grouping.

The bottleneck is I/O scheduling, not GEMM kernels. But scheduling lives in the runtime — which is why this project stopped trying to be one.

This also explains, quantitatively, why the current tools fail. llama.cpp with a 17 GB model on 7.6 GB of RAM falls back to demand paging through mmap: synchronous faults, 4–64 KiB granularity, queue depth 1. That is the 0.12 GB/s cell. AirLLM's layer streaming is honest about the result — a single response takes 15 to 30 minutes. Neither is failing because the disk is slow. They are failing because they use it at 5% of its rated speed.

Nobody has built the engine for the configuration most people actually own: no discrete GPU, single-digit gigabytes of RAM, and a fast SSD with hundreds of gigabytes free.

04

The idea: speculation as a bandwidth amplifier

Speculative decoding is universally described as a latency trick: a small draft model proposes k tokens, the big model verifies them in one pass, and you win because the big model's forward pass was memory-bound and had compute to spare.

In this setting that framing undersells it badly. Verifying k tokens together does three things at once, and the second and third are what matter here:

  • It amortizes each expert read across k tokens instead of one.
  • Because adjacent tokens reuse experts — measured overlap runs about 2× a random-routing baseline, with ~44% of routers selecting the same expert as the previous block — the union of experts needed by a block of k tokens is far smaller than k times the per-token set. Reads grow sublinearly in k.
  • Most importantly, knowing the whole block's expert set up front lets you issue every read for that block concurrently. That is where the queue depth comes from — the measured 3×.

The draft model's real job is not to save compute. It is to see far enough ahead to keep the NVMe queue full.

05

Four ideas, ranked by expected leverage

The first is a recomposition of known parts aimed at a configuration nobody targets. The other three I have not found in the literature, and each is falsifiable on its own.

01

Speculation as a queue-depth generator — recomposition

Run the draft model far enough ahead that the fetch scheduler always has a full block of expert requests to issue. Speculation depth becomes a tunable that trades draft compute for I/O parallelism, and the optimum is set by the measured QD curve rather than by acceptance rate alone.

Falsifiable claim: effective read bandwidth during decode reaches ≥2.0 GB/s sustained, versus ≤0.8 GB/s for a demand-paged baseline on the same model and machine.

prior art SpecOffload and Zhang et al. combine speculation with offloading, but both assume a GPU with idle compute and offload to host RAM. The CPU-only, disk-resident case inverts the economics: draft compute is expensive and the thing being bought is queue depth, not spare FLOPs.

02

Co-activation-aware disk layout — novel, and directly measured

Expert order on disk is normally an accident of the checkpoint's tensor order. Treat it as a graph partitioning problem instead: run a calibration corpus, build the expert co-activation matrix, and pack experts that fire together into 2 MiB aligned groups. One read then serves several needed experts, and the access pattern moves from the 0.12 GB/s corner of the table to the 2.41 GB/s corner.

Falsifiable claim: on a fine-grained MoE (128+ experts/layer), co-activation packing delivers ≥2× effective read bandwidth over checkpoint order at identical hit rate — a pure layout win, no accuracy change whatsoever.

prior art Oracle-MoE and Sticky Routing change routing to improve locality; ReMoE fine-tunes the router. All modify the model. This modifies only the byte layout of the file, so it composes with every one of them and risks nothing.

03

Bit allocation by access frequency, not just sensitivity — novel

Mixed-precision quantization allocates bits by sensitivity: how much loss rises if this tensor is crushed. That objective is wrong when the binding constraint is bytes moved through a tiered store. The right objective weights each expert by how often it is actually read and by which tier serves it — a cold expert costs 12× more per byte than a resident one.

bits(e) ∝ argmin over budget of Σ freq(e) · Δloss(e, bits)  s.t.  Σ freq(e) · bytes(e) · cost(tier(e)) ≤ budget

In practice: hot resident experts get 5–6 bits, cold disk experts get 2–3. Expected loss barely moves because rarely-read experts contribute little to average loss, while cold-path bytes — the expensive ones — roughly halve.

prior art Every mixed-precision scheme I found (SpQR, per-layer bit search, sensitivity-guided allocation) optimizes accuracy per byte stored. None optimizes accuracy per byte moved through a specific memory tier, which is the quantity that sets tokens per second here.

04

The personalization flywheel — novel as a deployed loop

A local engine has something no cloud service has: the complete trace of one user's actual workload. Every night, cheaply and offline, use it — (a) re-partition the disk layout against the observed co-activation matrix; (b) re-allocate bits against observed frequencies; (c) fine-tune the router with a temporal-locality penalty so experts persist across adjacent tokens, raising cache hit rate; (d) distil the draft model on the big model's own accepted tokens for this user, raising acceptance and therefore block size.

Falsifiable claim: tokens/sec on a held-out slice of the user's own workload improves ≥30% between day 1 and day 14, with no change to the base checkpoint and no measurable quality regression.

prior art ReMoE and Sticky Routing establish that router fine-tuning for reuse works, but as a training-time step against a general corpus. Domain-specific pruning literature personalizes to a domain. Nothing closes the loop on one user's live traces as a continuous deployment-time process — which is the only setting where a per-user objective is legitimate.

07

Why Rust, specifically

You asked for the most efficient and secure language. For this workload those two requirements point at the same answer, but the security argument is much stronger than the usual abstract one — because in a local inference engine the untrusted input is the model file itself, downloaded from a public hub and parsed by hand-written code full of offset arithmetic.

That surface is not hypothetical. It has been repeatedly and recently exploited:

Both are pure parser bugs in the format layer — the part of an inference engine with no performance requirement at all, where C++ buys nothing and costs everything. In safe Rust, the integer overflow is a checked-arithmetic panic and the out-of-bounds read is a bounds-check panic. The class of bug simply does not reach exploitability.

This design pushes that further into a hard architectural rule, because Windlass is more exposed than llama.cpp, not less: it rewrites model files on disk and does asynchronous DMA-adjacent I/O into aligned buffers.

Safe Rust, no exceptionsformat & control planeGGUF/safetensors parsing, the layout rewriter, the fetch scheduler, cache policy, the HTTP/RPC surface. Every length and offset from a file is validated before use; #![forbid(unsafe_code)] at the crate level.
Audited unsafe, quarantinedtwo crates onlywl-kernels (AVX2/AVX-VNNI intrinsics for int4×int8 dot products) and wl-io (aligned buffers, io_uring submission). Each unsafe block carries a written invariant; both crates run under Miri and a fuzz corpus in CI.

The efficiency side is straightforward: no GC pauses in a latency-sensitive decode loop, no runtime to ship, a single static binary, std::arch intrinsics that reach the same instructions as hand-written C, and the best async I/O story of any systems language — which matters more here than SIMD, since §02 showed the bottleneck is queue management. The ecosystem is also ready: mistral.rs and candle are competitive with llama.cpp on CPU today, so tensor plumbing and GGUF schema work can be borrowed rather than rewritten.

The core of the scheduler — the piece that turns lookahead into queue depth — is about this much code:

// wl-sched: one speculative block becomes one batch of concurrent reads.
pub async fn prefetch_block(&self, draft: &[TokenId], layer: LayerIdx) -> Result<()> {
    // 1. predict the expert set for every token in the block, then union it.
    let mut want = ExpertSet::new();
    for t in draft { want.extend(self.predictor.top_k(layer, t)?); }

    // 2. drop resident experts; map the rest to 2 MiB co-activation groups,
    //    so one read can satisfy several misses at once.
    let groups: BTreeSet<GroupId> = want.iter()
        .filter(|e| !self.cache.resident(e))
        .map(|e| self.store.group_of(e))
        .collect();

    // 3. submit every read at once. this is the whole trick: QD == groups.len(),
    //    which the measured curve turns into ~3x the bandwidth of QD1.
    let reads = groups.iter().map(|g| self.io.read_group(*g));
    for buf in futures::future::try_join_all(reads).await? {
        self.cache.admit(buf, AdmitCost::from_tier(Tier::Nvme));
    }
    Ok(())
}

09

Build order, each stage with a kill criterion

Ordered so that the cheapest experiments test the load-bearing assumptions first. If a kill criterion trips, the honest move is to stop and fall back to §03's last row — a small dense model with test-time compute and tools.

M0

Roofline probe done

Measure DRAM bandwidth and the NVMe blob×QD surface on the target machine. Already built and run — it produced §02 and fixed the 2 MiB / QD8 design point.

kill if best-case random read is under ~1 GB/s, or the DRAM:NVMe ratio exceeds ~40×. Neither held.

M1

Store format and layout rewriter

wl-format + wl-forge: read a GGUF MoE checkpoint, trace routing over a calibration corpus, build the co-activation matrix, partition into 2 MiB groups, write the Windlass store. Pure offline work, no inference loop needed — and it tests idea 02 in isolation by replaying a recorded trace against both layouts.

kill if co-activation packing yields under 1.5× effective bandwidth over checkpoint order on a replayed trace.

M2

Correct-but-slow engine, instrumented

wl-kernels + wl-engine: single-token decode, no speculation, everything measured. The goal is not speed — it is ground truth on the two assumptions the projection rests on: the real cache hit rate at a 3.5 GB budget, and the real expert overlap between adjacent tokens.

kill if measured hit rate falls below ~40% or adjacent-token expert overlap is near the random baseline. Either one collapses the projection.

M3

Speculative loop and the fetch scheduler

wl-sched: draft model, per-layer router predictors, block union, io_uring prefetch. This is where the headline number is either won or lost. Tune k against the measured QD curve rather than against acceptance rate.

kill if sustained decode-time read bandwidth stays under 1.5 GB/s despite deep queues.

M4

Cost-aware bit allocation

Frequency×tier-weighted bit search in wl-forge, validated against a quality suite so the accuracy cost of crushing cold experts is measured, not assumed.

kill if 2-bit cold experts cost more than ~1 point on the evaluation suite. Fall back to uniform 4-bit and accept roughly half the throughput.

M5

The nightly flywheel

Trace collection, re-layout, router locality fine-tune, draft distillation on accepted tokens. Strictly last: it is the most speculative idea and it multiplies whatever M1–M4 achieved rather than enabling it.

kill if day-14 throughput on held-out user workload improves under 10%. Ship without it; the engine still stands.

10

What could kill this

Stated plainly, because the failure modes are specific and worth knowing before you write M1.

  • SSD write endurance. Reads are free; the nightly re-layout is not. Rewriting a 60 GB store often enough would matter on a consumer drive. Mitigation: rewrite incrementally, only groups whose partition actually changed, and cap it at a fixed budget per week.
  • Router predictor accuracy is the whole game. Prefetching the wrong experts is worse than not prefetching — you spend bandwidth and still stall. If per-layer prediction cannot clear roughly 80% top-k recall a few layers ahead, the queue depth argument fails. This is the single most important thing M2 measures.
  • Thermals on a 15 W laptop chip. Sustained 12-thread AVX-VNNI on a U-series part will throttle. The projection's compute headroom is comfortable enough to absorb it, but sustained throughput will sit below burst.
  • WSL2 taxes both bottlenecks. The 2.41 GB/s measurement already includes virtual-disk overhead, so bare-metal Linux should be better, not worse — but iGPU access via /dev/dxg runs Vulkan at roughly two-thirds of native, so treat any GPU path as a bonus, never as load-bearing.
  • Dense models get nothing from this. The design is fundamentally about sparsity. A dense 70B remains hopeless here, and honestly so — no layout or scheduling trick changes the fact that it touches every weight for every token.

If the sparsity assumption holds, this works. If it doesn't, no amount of engineering saves it — and M2 tells you which within days.
